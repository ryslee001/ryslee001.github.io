HCDE 313 · User Research · Spring 2026

Leaving your stuff behind: trust and safety in campus study spaces

How can we help students feel safe temporarily leaving their belongings unattended in UW academic spaces?

RoleTeam lead, researcher
Team4 researchers
Duration10 weeks
ToolsFigma, Excel, Google Forms

The problem

Students regularly leave a laptop, backpack or phone at their desk to grab coffee or use the restroom. Duke, the University of Illinois and Bentley have all published warnings telling students not to, and students ask on Reddit and Quora whether it's safe. So the worry clearly isn't limited to one campus.

No one had studied how safe students actually feel doing it, or what shapes that feeling. That's what we set out to learn.

Process

01Field studyObserved students in Odegaard, Suzzallo, Allen and the Hans Rosling Center in 30–60 min sessions
02Affinity mappingCleaned and labeled field notes, then grouped them in Figma to find patterns
03InterviewsSemi-structured, 15–30 min, with students who use these spaces
04SurveyA follow-up survey to check whether the interview patterns held up across more students

I led the team through planning, wrote the study's research questions, and helped with observation, interviewing and synthesis. We picked the four sites because they differ on purpose: one student-only library that requires ID to enter, two quiet public libraries, and a bright mixed-use building with a café.

Research questions

  • What environmental cues already exist that make students feel safe?
  • How long are belongings typically left behind?
  • How do students show a seat is taken when they're not in it?
  • What factors most strongly influence whether a student feels safe leaving their things?

Key findings

1. Students trust each other more than they trust security measures

In the ID-gated library, students left open laptops and backpacks out with no visible concern. In interviews, they told us safety comes from knowing the people around them are fellow students, an unspoken agreement that we're all in this together.

“If the people around me are students or faculty, I feel it's a safe environment, so I'll leave my stuff.”

2. Warning signs didn't change behavior

Allen Library was the only site with theft-warning signs on every desk, but students there behaved the same as students everywhere else. Signs alone don't make people feel safer or act differently.

3. Students worry about losing their seat, not just about theft

The interviews showed us something the observation couldn't. Students use their belongings to show a seat is taken, so packing everything up means risking the spot. When the space gets crowded, like during finals week, some students won't leave at all.

“On a busy day, like during finals week, I worry someone might clear my stuff to take the spot, so I try not to leave at all.”

4. Students rank their belongings by importance

Wallets, phones and laptops stay with students. Jackets are left to hold a seat, but other students don't always respect that. People need a way to save a seat that doesn't put anything valuable at risk.

Recommendations

In-desk locker

A small compartment at each desk that unlocks with a Husky Card tap. It keeps valuables secure, and a locked compartment also shows the seat is taken.

“Be right back” signal

A small light or flip-tab on the desk so the seat clearly looks occupied without the student leaving anything behind.

Pilot during finals

Test first in Odegaard during midterms and finals, when seats are hardest to find, then expand to other buildings.

One participant's pushback changed our design. A shared bank of lockers would be “kind of a hassle… just to go to the bathroom for like two minutes,” so we put the storage within arm's reach, at the desk itself.

Reflection

Observation tells you what people do but not why. In the field, we couldn't correlate the setting with how safe students felt. The interviews connected the two: students feel safe because of who else is in the space, not because of any physical security feature.

If I ran this study again, I'd observe the same spaces at different times, such as finals week, and for longer sessions, since 30 minutes misses behavior that repeats.
