Leaving your stuff behind: trust and safety in campus study spaces

How can we help students feel safe temporarily leaving their belongings unattended in UW academic spaces?

RoleTeam lead, researcher
Team4 researchers
Duration10 weeks
MethodsField study, interviews, survey

The problem

Students regularly leave a laptop, backpack or phone at their desk to grab coffee or use the restroom. Duke, the University of Illinois and Bentley have all published warnings telling students not to, and students ask on Reddit and Quora whether it's safe. So the worry clearly isn't limited to one campus.

No one had studied how safe students actually feel doing it, or what shapes that feeling. Over one quarter, we used three methods, each building on the last: observation to see what students do, interviews to learn why, and a survey to check whether the patterns held across more students.

My role

  • Led the team through planning and kept the three studies connected, so each one picked up the open questions from the one before.
  • Wrote the field study's research questions.
  • Observed the 2nd floor of Odegaard Library and Suzzallo Library.
  • Conducted and transcribed one of the four interviews.
  • Built the team's thematic analysis board in FigJam, turning interview quotes into codes and themes.

Field study

Weeks 2–4 · Observation at 4 sites

Each of us observed a different space for 30–60 minutes. We picked the four sites because they differ on purpose:

  • Odegaard Library: student-only, Husky Card required to enter, multiple floors and study rooms
  • Suzzallo Library: quiet and open to the public, mostly individual seating
  • Allen Library: two connected buildings, quiet floors, plus a livelier ground-floor collaboration space
  • Hans Rosling Center: bright, mixed classroom and study building with a coffee shop

We sat near the back of each space to see as much of the room as possible. For every student who stepped away, we noted what they left, how they arranged it, whether they told anyone, and how long they were gone. We typed notes live, cleaned them and labeled them by area of focus. We recorded behavior and general appearance only, never names or identifying details, and we didn't interact with anyone.

What we saw

  • Most students left valuables in plain sight. Open laptops, tablets and backpacks were the norm, especially in ID-gated Odegaard.
  • Most absences were short. Typically 5–6 minutes, likely the restroom. Some students stayed away for 10+ minutes or past the end of the session.
  • Group studiers asked someone to watch their things. Solo studiers almost never did, no matter how long they'd be gone.
  • Warning signs didn't change behavior. Allen had theft-warning signs on every desk, yet students there acted the same as everywhere else.

The open question was why. Observation couldn't tell us whether students felt safe or just didn't think about it. That's what the interviews were for.

Interviews

Weeks 5–7 · 4 semi-structured interviews

Each of us interviewed one UW student who regularly or occasionally uses these spaces. We excluded anyone who couldn't recall studying in them. Sessions ran 15–30 minutes, on campus. The protocol moved from concrete to reflective:

  1. Share a time you had to step away and leave your belongings behind.
  2. Walk me through what you typically do when you step away.
  3. Which belongings would you leave behind, and which would you never leave?
  4. When you see someone else's things left at a spot, what does it signal to you?
  5. Have you ever used anything to show others you're coming back? How did that go?

Question 4 flips the perspective on purpose. Saving a seat only works if other students read the signal the same way, so we needed both sides.

What we learned

  • Being a UW student works as a trust signal. Students feel safe because the people around them are other students. That explains why Odegaard's ID gate mattered more than Allen's signs.
  • Students don't just fear theft, they also fear losing their seat. Belongings are how students hold a seat, so packing up means giving the spot away.
  • Students rank their belongings. Wallets, phones and laptops stay with them. Jackets hold a seat, but other students don't always respect that.
  • Students proposed their own solutions. One suggested “a locker right at the desk… lock it with your student ID.” Another pushed back on shared lockers: “kind of a hassle… just to go to the bathroom for like two minutes.”

Four interviews gave us depth but not numbers. We wrote the survey to test these patterns with more students.

Survey

Weeks 8–10 · 12-question survey · 17 respondents

We built a 12-question Google Form and recruited UW students who study alone in campus spaces through HCDE communities and by email. Responses were anonymous. Each question was tied to an interview finding we wanted to confirm (is it common?), elaborate (why?), or extend (would a design help?). The survey mixed multiple-choice, 1–5 rating, ranking and open-ended questions.

Other results

  • Crowds split students. 7 felt more comfortable in a busy space (more people around), while 5 felt less comfortable (their seat might get taken). Crowd density cuts both ways, which matches the interviews.
  • Asking a stranger isn't a real solution. Comfort averaged 2.8 out of 5.
  • Interest in our designs was real but moderate. In-desk lockers averaged 3.4 out of 5 (7 rated them 4–5), and a seat indicator averaged 3.2 (8 rated it 4–5). Neither was a slam dunk, so any solution has to take almost no effort to use.
  • Theft is still the biggest worry. When asked to name their biggest concern, most respondents described theft or losing their things. Two mentioned items being moved or their seat being taken.
  • In their own words, students described the same rule we'd seen: “I leave everything behind when I'm in Odegaard since people need to show their Husky card to get in.”

Recommendations

In-desk locker

A small compartment at each desk that unlocks with a Husky Card tap. It keeps valuables secure, and a locked compartment also shows the seat is taken, so students don't have to choose between the two.

“Be right back” signal

A small light or flip-tab on the desk. 8 of 17 already rank a note or sign as the clearest seat-saver, so a standard version can replace leaving a laptop out.

Pilot during finals

Start in Odegaard during midterms and finals, when seats are scarcest and students are most afraid to leave. Expand to other buildings if it works.

Both designs use the Husky Card on purpose. Being a UW student was the strongest source of trust across all three studies, so the solution should build on that trust, not replace it with a new system.

Reflection

Combining methods paid off. Observation showed what students did, interviews explained why, and the survey showed which patterns were common. Odegaard's ID gate is a good example: we noticed it in the field, students explained it in the interviews, and 16 of 17 survey respondents confirmed it.

Limitations:

  • Observation sessions were 30–60 minutes, too short to see the same student come and go more than once.
  • Seventeen respondents, recruited mostly through HCDE channels, can confirm a direction but not represent all of UW.
  • We only studied regular weeks.

Next time, I'd observe the same spaces during finals week. That's when seat scarcity peaks and students said they're least willing to leave.
